# Economic Efficiency When Prices Are Observed

A production frontier describes what an organization could attain with the
technology represented in the data. It does not, by itself, identify the economically
preferred plan. Prices make that comparison possible, but only after management has
said which parts of the current operation are commitments and which parts can change.

A hospital may have to deliver a fixed bundle of services and seek the least costly
way to do so. A university may have fixed staff and facilities and ask which mix of
teaching and research creates the most value. A farm or factory with discretion over
both resources and products may instead seek the largest operating surplus. These are
not different names for one efficiency score. They are, respectively, cost, revenue,
and profit decisions.

This chapter develops those decisions as one production-economic family and shows
how technical and allocative performance fit together. A short final bridge connects
a declared directional programme to the profit gap without turning that bridge into
a fourth economic objective. The distinction between technical and allocative
performance originates in Farrell's account of productive efficiency
{cite:p}`farrell1957`; cost, revenue, profit, and distance measures can then be
understood within a common technology-and-prices framework {cite:p}`fare1985`.

## Begin with the economic decision

Let an organization use inputs

$$
x=(x_1,\ldots,x_m)^\top
$$

to produce desirable outputs

$$
y=(y_1,\ldots,y_s)^\top.
$$

The technology $\mathcal T$ contains the input--output plans regarded as attainable under the
study's data, reference-set policy, disposability assumptions, and returns to scale.
Let $w\in\mathbb R_{++}^m$ and $p\in\mathbb R_{++}^s$ denote input and output
prices. The observed accounts are

$$
C=w^\top x,\qquad
R=p^\top y,\qquad
\Pi=p^\top y-w^\top x.
$$

One technology and one set of prices therefore support three different
counterfactuals:

| Management question | What remains committed? | What may the benchmark change? | Economic result |
|---|---|---|---|
| How cheaply could the promised outputs be delivered? | At least the observed outputs | Input quantities and mix | Minimum cost |
| How much value could the available resources generate? | No more than the observed inputs | Output quantities and mix | Maximum revenue |
| Which attainable operation creates the largest net return? | Neither side of the observed plan | Inputs, outputs, and operating scale jointly | Maximum profit |

The commitment is the key to interpretation. A unit can be cost efficient for its
current output obligation yet have a large profit gap because another scale or output
portfolio would be more valuable. It can also use its existing capacity well while
purchasing an unnecessarily expensive input mix. Prices do not alter technical
feasibility; they rank feasible plans. A change in relative prices can therefore
change the preferred benchmark even when the production frontier is unchanged.

## A service system for the whole chapter

Consider four attainable service plans. Each uses one composite resource to deliver
two services, one of which receives a higher valuation.

| Plan | Resource $x$ | Standard service $y_1$ | Specialist service $y_2$ |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| A | 4 | 6 | 2 |
| B | 5 | 4 | 5 |
| C | 3 | 5 | 1 |
| D | 6 | 3 | 2 |

One unit of the resource costs 2, and the services are valued at 3 and 5:

$$
w=2,\qquad p=(3,5)^\top.
$$

The observed economic accounts are consequently:

| Plan | Cost | Revenue | Profit |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| A | 8 | 28 | 20 |
| B | 10 | 37 | 27 |
| C | 6 | 20 | 14 |
| D | 12 | 19 | 7 |

We use the variable-returns-to-scale (VRS) convex technology generated by A--D, with
ordinary free disposal. A benchmark may be an observed plan or a convex combination
of plans. Plan D is the main management case. Its observed account is weak, but each
model will explain that weakness against a different, decision-relevant alternative.
Plans A and C will show why technical, conditional economic, and joint profit
performance cannot be treated as interchangeable labels.

## Cost: protect the service commitment

Cost analysis is appropriate when the organization must deliver at least its observed
outputs but can adjust the resources used. Examples include a contracted production
volume, a regulated service obligation, or a fixed order book.

For unit $o$, let $X$ and $Y$ collect the reference inputs and outputs. Minimum
attainable cost is

$$
\begin{aligned}
C_o^*(w_o,y_o)
=\min_{\lambda}\quad & w_o^\top X\lambda\\
\text{s.t.}\quad
&Y\lambda\ge y_o,\\
&\lambda\ge0,
\end{aligned}
$$

with the declared returns-to-scale restriction. Under VRS,
$\mathbf 1^\top\lambda=1$; under constant returns to scale (CRS), that equality is
absent. The chosen activity is the attainable plan that satisfies the output
commitment at the lowest bill, not necessarily the physically nearest plan.

Two reports serve different purposes:

$$
CE_o=\frac{C_o^*}{C_o},
\qquad
G_o^C=C_o-C_o^*.
$$

Cost efficiency $CE_o$ is a dimensionless ratio, with one best for a positive-price,
self-inclusive technology. The cost gap $G_o^C$ expresses the same opportunity in
money units and is often more useful for budgeting.

### Technical and allocative sources of a cost gap

An input-oriented Farrell model holds outputs fixed and contracts the observed input
bundle in common proportion. Let its input technical efficiency be
$TE_o^I=\theta_o$. Cost allocative efficiency is conventionally

$$
AE_o^C=\frac{CE_o}{TE_o^I},
\qquad
CE_o=TE_o^I AE_o^C.
$$

The first component values the opportunity to reduce the existing resource bundle
proportionally. The second records the remaining cost advantage when the input mix
may respond to relative prices. The same distinction can be written as a monetary
account:

$$
\underbrace{C_o-C_o^*}_{\text{total cost opportunity}}
=
\underbrace{C_o-\theta_oC_o}_{\text{proportional resource saving}}
+
\underbrace{\theta_oC_o-C_o^*}_{\text{remaining cost saving}}.
$$

The word *allocative* requires care. If the radial technical plan retains input
slacks, the remaining cost component can combine non-proportional resource savings
with price-responsive input substitution. An output surplus does not enter the input
bill directly, although it prevents the radial plan from supporting an unconditional
strong-efficiency claim. The identity remains exact, but the second component should
not automatically be interpreted as purchasing skill.

For Plan D, the least-cost activity is $0.25B+0.75C$. It uses 3.5 units of the
resource and produces $(4.75,2)$, so it satisfies D's service commitment at a cost of
7. Therefore

$$
CE_D=\frac{7}{12}\approx0.583,
\qquad
G_D^C=12-7=5.
$$

With only one input there is no composition to change, so
$TE_D^I=7/12$ and $AE_D^C=1$. This does not make D a good operation overall. It says
that its five-unit cost opportunity is fully explained by resource contraction in
this one-input example. Nor must the least-cost activity reproduce outputs exactly:
free disposal permits it to exceed the commitment for standard services.

## Revenue: use the available capacity well

Revenue analysis treats the observed input vector as the resource envelope and asks
which feasible output quantities and mix create the greatest value at $p_o$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
R_o^*(p_o,x_o)
=\max_{\lambda}\quad & p_o^\top Y\lambda\\
\text{s.t.}\quad
&X\lambda\le x_o,\\
&\lambda\ge0,
\end{aligned}
$$

again with the chosen returns-to-scale restriction. Revenue efficiency and its
money-valued gap are

$$
RE_o=\frac{R_o}{R_o^*},
\qquad
G_o^R=R_o^*-R_o.
$$

A revenue ratio of 0.70 means that the observed portfolio earns 70 percent of the
largest revenue supported by the same input envelope. That interpretation assumes
the supplied prices remain meaningful for the additional outputs. Demand limits,
volume-sensitive prices, quotas, and changing quality can otherwise make the
counterfactual commercially unrealistic.

Let $\phi_o\ge1$ be the greatest common-proportion expansion of observed desirable
outputs supported by the input envelope. On a higher-is-better scale,
$TE_o^O=1/\phi_o$, and

$$
AE_o^R=\frac{RE_o}{TE_o^O},
\qquad
RE_o=TE_o^OAE_o^R.
$$

$TE_o^O$ asks whether more of the existing output bundle could be delivered;
$AE_o^R$ records the remaining advantage of changing its composition in response to
output values. If the radial plan retains desirable-output slacks, the remaining
revenue component can combine non-proportional service gains with price-responsive
output reallocation. An input saving does not enter the revenue account directly,
although it still prevents an unconditional strong-efficiency conclusion.

Plan D earns 19 with six units of resource. Plan B uses only five units and earns 37,
so

$$
RE_D=\frac{19}{37}\approx0.514,
\qquad
G_D^R=37-19=18.
$$

The greatest proportional expansion of D's observed output mix is
$\phi_D=22/13$, giving

$$
TE_D^O=\frac{13}{22}\approx0.591,
\qquad
AE_D^R
=\frac{19/37}{13/22}
=\frac{418}{481}
\approx0.869.
$$

Part of D's revenue shortfall is thus associated with delivering too little from its
resource envelope; another part is associated with its low-value output mix. Plan A
provides the converse lesson. It cannot expand both outputs proportionally, so
$TE_A^O=1$, yet $0.5B+0.5C$ uses the same input and earns 28.5 rather than 28. Hence

$$
RE_A=AE_A^R=\frac{28}{28.5}=\frac{56}{57}<1.
$$

A can be technically efficient for proportional output expansion without being
revenue maximizing.

## Profit: choose the operating plan jointly

Cost and revenue analysis protect one side of the observed operation. Profit analysis
removes both conditional commitments and asks which attainable input--output plan
creates the largest difference between revenue and cost. For the VRS technology,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Pi_o^*(w_o,p_o)
=\max_{\lambda}\quad
&p_o^\top Y\lambda-w_o^\top X\lambda\\
\text{s.t.}\quad
&\mathbf 1^\top\lambda=1,\\
&\lambda\ge0.
\end{aligned}
$$

The natural measure of profit inefficiency is the raw gap

$$
G_o^\Pi=\Pi_o^*-\Pi_o.
$$

Profit is already a difference between two accounts. Observed or maximum profit can
be zero or negative, so a ratio can become undefined or reverse the economic
ranking. The raw gap remains meaningful: it is the additional profit available
within the declared technology and at the supplied prices.

| Result | Formula | Best value | Unit |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Cost efficiency | $C_o^*/C_o$ | 1 | Ratio |
| Revenue efficiency | $R_o/R_o^*$ | 1 | Ratio |
| Profit gap | $\Pi_o^*-\Pi_o$ | 0 | Currency or declared value |

In the example, Plan B maximizes profit:

$$
\Pi^*=\max\{20,27,14,7\}=27,
$$

so the gaps for A--D are $(7,0,13,20)$. Plan D has a twenty-unit profit opportunity.
Plan C illustrates why this is not redundant with the conditional models. It is both
cost efficient for its output commitment and revenue efficient within its three-unit
input envelope, but it has a profit gap of 13 because profit maximization permits a
different scale and service portfolio. “Best conditional on the current operation”
is not the same as “best attainable operation at these prices.”

## A directional bridge to the profit gap

The profit gap measures the whole economic opportunity. Sometimes management also
wants to know how much of that opportunity is represented by a *declared operating
programme*. This is where the directional distance function from the preceding
chapter meets the price account; it is not a fourth production technology or a
parallel management objective {cite:p}`chambers1998,zofio2013profit`.

Suppose one unit of the programme saves $g_o^x$ inputs and adds $g_o^y$ outputs. If
the directional model supports $\beta_o$ units of that package, prices value one
programme unit at

$$
\nu_o=w_o^\top g_o^x+p_o^\top g_o^y>0.
$$

The feasible programme therefore accounts for $\nu_o\beta_o$ of the profit
opportunity. Under the same technology, prices, returns to scale, and reference set,
that amount cannot exceed the total profit gap. The Nerlovian normalization simply
expresses the gap in programme units:

$$
NI_o:=\frac{G_o^\Pi}{\nu_o}
=
\beta_o+\frac{G_o^\Pi-\nu_o\beta_o}{\nu_o}.
$$

The residual is the profit opportunity not represented by the selected package. It
may reflect a different input mix, output mix, operating scale, or technical changes
outside the declared direction; calling it “allocative” does not prove a managerial
cause.

Some literature calls the normalized quantity *Nerlovian efficiency*. Because
$NI_o$ is zero at the profit optimum and rises with foregone profit, this book and
DEAPack use *Nerlovian profit inefficiency*. `NerlovianEfficiency` is retained only
as a discoverability alias; it does not define another model.

This reconciliation is reported only when both underlying decisions can be rebuilt:
the selected activity must attain the stated profit maximum, and the directional
activity must actually deliver $\beta_o$ units of the same declared programme within
the same reference technology. A finite optimum without those two economic accounts
cannot support the decomposition.

For Plan D, the package “save one resource unit and add one unit of each service” is
worth 10. The directional model supports 1.6 packages, while the full profit gap is
20. Thus the programme represents 16 of the opportunity and 4 remains. This compact
bridge is the substantive lesson. Detailed direction-normalization choices and the
complete `NerlovianProfitInefficiency` field inventory belong in the DEAPack
Documentation.

## Reproduce the account with DEAPack

The core case uses one data object, one price contract, and one VRS technology.
Keeping these choices common is what makes the cost, revenue, and profit comparisons
coherent.

```python
from deapack import (
    AllocativeDecomposition,
    DEAData,
    NerlovianProfitInefficiency,
    PriceData,
    ProfitEfficiency,
    RevenueAllocativeDecomposition,
    dataset_info,
    load_dataset,
)

frame = load_dataset("economic_efficiency_4")
roles = dataset_info("economic_efficiency_4").roles
data = DEAData.from_frame(
    frame,
    dmu=roles["dmu"],
    inputs=roles["inputs"],
    outputs=roles["outputs"],
)
prices = PriceData.common(
    input_prices={"resource": 2.0},
    output_prices={
        "standard_service": 3.0,
        "premium_service": 5.0,
    },
)

cost = AllocativeDecomposition(returns_to_scale="vrs").fit(data, prices)
revenue = RevenueAllocativeDecomposition(returns_to_scale="vrs").fit(data, prices)
profit = ProfitEfficiency(returns_to_scale="vrs").fit(data, prices)
nerlovian = NerlovianProfitInefficiency(
    input_direction={"resource": 1.0},
    output_direction={
        "standard_service": 1.0,
        "premium_service": 1.0,
    },
).fit(data, prices)

cost_table = cost.summary().set_index("dmu_id")
revenue_table = revenue.summary().set_index("dmu_id")
profit_table = profit.summary().set_index("dmu_id")
nerlovian_table = nerlovian.summary().set_index("dmu_id")

print(cost_table.loc["D", [
    "minimum_cost", "cost_efficiency",
    "technical_efficiency", "allocative_efficiency",
]])
print(revenue_table.loc["D", [
    "maximum_revenue", "revenue_efficiency",
    "technical_efficiency", "allocative_efficiency",
]])
print(profit_table.loc["D", ["maximum_profit", "profit_gap"]])
print(nerlovian_table.loc["D", [
    "direction_value", "nerlovian_inefficiency",
    "technical_inefficiency", "allocative_inefficiency",
]])
```

The four summaries reproduce the core economic account and its directional bridge:

| Question for Plan D | Result | Decision-relevant reading |
|---|---:|---|
| Minimum cost for $(3,2)$ | $7$; $CE=TE^I AE^C=(7/12)(1)$ | The current service commitment could be delivered for 5 less |
| Maximum revenue with at most 6 units of the resource | $37$; $RE=TE^O AE^R=(13/22)(418/481)$ | The same resource envelope supports 18 more value units |
| Maximum profit | $27$; gap $=20$ | Joint adjustment favors Plan B over D |
| Price-valued declared programme | $\nu=10$; $NI=2.0=1.6+0.4$ | The programme represents 16 of the 20-unit profit opportunity |

Before any of these numbers becomes a management claim, the economic account must
reconcile with the benchmark operation. Minimum cost must equal the input-price bill
of the selected cost plan, maximum revenue must equal the output value of the
selected revenue plan, and maximum profit must equal benchmark revenue minus
benchmark cost. DEAPack verifies those links and withholds an unreconciled result.
This safeguard cannot make the prices or the reference population economically
credible; it establishes only that the reported account follows from the declared
model and benchmark.

Headline values should be read with the associated benchmark activity, peers, and
slacks. A minimum-cost activity, a maximum-revenue activity, and a profit-maximizing
activity are different counterfactuals even when two of them happen to select the
same observed peer. A directional programme can then be used to interpret part of
the profit gap; it does not replace these three decisions.

## Prices and technology form one empirical contract

Economic DEA results are meaningful only when both sides of the comparison are
credible. Prices define the value account; the technology defines the attainable
alternatives.

### Prices are observed valuations, not flexible DEA weights

The vectors $w$ and $p$ are wage rates, rental prices, tariffs, product prices,
reimbursements, or explicitly justified social values supplied by the analyst. The
study must also say whose valuation they represent. Market prices may describe a
firm's commercial opportunity, administered tariffs may describe a regulator's
payment regime, and social values may express a public decision-maker's priorities;
none is automatically a measure of social welfare. These observed valuations are not
the endogenous multiplier weights through which a conventional DEA model values each
unit favorably. A technically efficient unit can still be far from the least-cost or
maximum-profit plan under the valuation regime it actually faces.

Weight-restricted DEA lies between unrestricted self-valuation and an observed-price
account. An authority may bound relative multipliers to express defensible service
priorities or production trade-offs, but those bounds remain declared value judgements;
they do not become wages, tariffs, or market prices. The resulting assessment therefore
belongs to its parent technical-efficiency family and must not be labelled cost,
revenue, or profit efficiency merely because its multipliers are restricted
{cite:p}`allen1997,cooper2011handbook`.

Variable names and physical units must match their prices. If labor is converted
from hours to thousands of hours, the price must be converted from currency per hour
to currency per thousand hours so that $w^\top x$ remains unchanged. Monetary
comparisons also require a common currency and price base. Deflation and conversion
belong in the study design, not in an interpretation added after estimation.

For the classical ratios used here, prices are finite and strictly positive. A zero
or negative valuation may represent a subsidy, disposal liability, bad output, or
public service whose economic role needs to be modelled explicitly. Replacing it by
an arbitrary small positive number changes the question rather than solving it.

Multiplying every input price by the same positive constant leaves cost efficiency
unchanged but rescales the cost gap; the analogous statement holds for revenue.
Multiplying both input and output prices by the same constant rescales the profit gap
and the value of a directional programme together, leaving the programme-unit account
unchanged. Changes in *relative* prices are different: they can select a different
input mix, output portfolio, or profit-maximizing operation.

Common prices compare organizations under one valuation regime, as in a national
tariff. Observation-specific prices evaluate each organization under its own wage,
procurement, or reimbursement conditions. Both can be legitimate, but the latter
mixes production opportunities with different local price environments and should
not be presented as a common-price league table.

### The opportunity set must match the decision

All components of any technical--economic decomposition must use the same
observations, variables, returns to scale, disposability assumptions, and reference
policy. Combining VRS economic efficiency with a CRS technical component divides
results from different opportunity sets and has no stable economic meaning.

The familiar bounds $CE\le1$, $RE\le1$, and nonnegative gaps also rely on the
observed plan being feasible in the reference technology. An external, historical,
or leave-group-out frontier may not envelop the evaluated unit. A ratio above one or
a negative gap should then be read as evidence that the unit lies beyond that
benchmark, not forced back into the usual range.

Cost and revenue problems can be posed under CRS or VRS. Unconstrained CRS profit
maximization requires special care. If one feasible operation earns positive profit,
CRS permits it to be replicated indefinitely:

$$
p^\top(ty)-w^\top(tx)
=t\bigl(p^\top y-w^\top x\bigr)\longrightarrow\infty
\quad\text{as }t\to\infty.
$$

Unboundedness is then the correct economic conclusion. A finite CRS profit benchmark
requires capacity constraints, an equilibrium price condition, or another explicit
scale limit. Our worked price account and directional bridge use VRS because its
convexity condition bounds the operating scale.

VRS does not automatically include the zero-activity plan. If every observed plan
makes a loss, the model selects the least-loss-making attainable operation. That can
be appropriate for a hospital or regulated utility with a continuing-service
obligation. If a firm can close in the long run, inactivity, fixed commitments, exit
costs, and minimum-service requirements must be represented in the opportunity set.
Shutdown is an economic choice, not something to infer mechanically from a negative
observed profit.

## From a benchmark to a management conclusion

A scalar result is the beginning of an explanation, not the end. Radial technical
models preserve the observed mix during their proportional step and can leave
non-proportional savings or expansions. Such slacks qualify any claim that the
residual component is purely allocative {cite:p}`pastor2022benchmarking,aparicio2023essential`.

The same economic optimum may also be attainable through several peer combinations.
The optimum value can therefore be well determined while the operating target is not
unique. If implementation depends on a particular target, the study should examine
alternative optima, transition costs, and organizational constraints rather than
present one selected activity as the only recommendation.

The models support disciplined counterfactual statements: under the recorded prices
and VRS comparison technology, D could meet its service commitment for 5 less, earn
18 more from its current resource envelope, or gain 20 in profit through joint
adjustment. They do not establish that management caused the gaps or that the peer
plan can be copied without adaptation. Case mix, quality, regulation, demand,
geography, and omitted resources can all matter.

A responsible analysis therefore proceeds in this order:

1. state the operational commitment and decision horizon;
2. define the technology and comparison set;
3. document price meanings, units, provenance, and variation across units;
4. choose the cost, revenue, or profit question that matches the commitment;
5. if useful, declare a directional programme for interpreting part of a profit gap;
6. interpret the value alongside the benchmark activity, peers, and slacks; and
7. investigate constraints and adjustment costs that are absent from the model.

There is no virtue in estimating every member of the family when only one
counterfactual is credible. The central distinction is simple: cost efficiency values
resource adjustment while protecting outputs; revenue efficiency values output
adjustment while protecting inputs; and the profit gap values joint adjustment. A
declared directional programme may illuminate part of that gap, but it is subordinate
to the economic decision. Once the commitment is explicit, each result has a clear
economic meaning rather than being merely another score attached to a frontier.
